Revenue Physics is my go-to-market model: revenue moves when Direction and Force overcome Mass and Friction, and keeps moving only with Momentum. Every engagement tunes these five variables.

That's the whole idea. The rest of this essay is how I use it.

## Why physics

I'm not claiming go-to-market obeys equations. It doesn't. Buyers are people, budgets are politics, and timing is luck more often than anyone admits.

But physics gives me a vocabulary founders already trust. An engineer knows that pushing harder on a heavy load with a bad bearing mostly makes heat. Nobody needs a slide for that. When I tell a robotics founder a deal has too much mass, they nod. When I tell them to "improve conversion", they reach for their phone.

So I keep one line on the whiteboard:

<Callout label="Mnemonic">
Velocity ∝ (Direction × Force) ÷ (Mass × Friction), sustained by Momentum. A mental model, not an equation.
</Callout>

Nobody should plug numbers into it. Its job is to stop a team from fixing the wrong variable.

## The five variables

Each variable is one layer of go-to-market, and each has one diagnostic question. I score them from 1 to 5. A 1 is founder-only and ad hoc. A 3 is documented and partly repeatable. A 5 runs without the founder and is measured weekly.

### Direction: positioning

**The question:** can a buyer explain why you, in one sentence, with you out of the room?

At 1, every rep describes the product differently and the homepage could belong to any AI company. At 5, buyers repeat your pitch back to you, unprompted, in the language of their own problem.

The common mistake is positioning against competitors when the real alternative is a spreadsheet, a contractor, or nothing at all.

### Mass: pricing and packaging

**The question:** how heavy is the decision?

Mass is everything a buyer has to carry to say yes: the price, the contract length, the integration work, the risk of looking foolish in front of their boss. At 1, every deal is a custom quote and every pilot is free and endless. At 5, a champion can defend the price in one email, and the first paid step is small enough to approve this quarter.

The common mistake is treating price as a number instead of a structure. The shape of the deal matters more than the figure on it.

### Force: marketing and pipeline

**The question:** how much qualified attention do you create each week?

At 1, pipeline depends on the founder's network, and launches spike and fade. At 5, a handful of channels produce qualified conversations every week, whether or not anyone shipped something new.

The common mistake is measuring force by volume. Ten thousand impressions pointed the wrong way is still zero.

### Friction: workflows and systems

**The question:** where does a deal sit and wait?

At 1, nobody trusts the CRM, every security review starts from scratch, and handoffs lose context. At 5, every step from first call to signature has an owner, every document is ready before it's asked for, and nobody waits on an answer that already exists.

The common mistake is buying more pipeline to make up for a leaky process. You end up paying twice for the same deal.

### Momentum: team and training

**The question:** does it still work when the founder's on a plane?

At 1, nothing closes while the founder is away. At 5, there is an operating rhythm, regular call reviews, a dashboard people actually read, and one person who owns the weekly number.

The common mistake is hiring a senior seller to create momentum. Momentum comes from a system a person can run, not from the person.

## Law 1: Inertia

<PullQuote>Your biggest competitor is the buyer doing nothing.</PullQuote>

Most deals I've seen lost weren't lost to a competitor. They were lost to "not this quarter". The buyer's current way of working has years of habit behind it. It's free, it's familiar, and nobody gets fired for keeping it.

That changes what positioning has to do. Before you explain why you beat another vendor, explain why staying put is expensive. Name the cost of the status quo in the buyer's own numbers. If you can't, you haven't earned the right to talk about features.

## Law 2: Mass

Heavy decisions need more force or less mass. Most teams add force. The faster fix is removing mass.

When a deal stalls, the instinct is to push: more calls, more follow-ups, a bigger discount at the end of the quarter. That's force. It works sometimes, and it's expensive every time.

Removing mass is usually faster. Four ways I do it:

- **Make the first step smaller.** One site, one team, one workflow. Expand after the proof, not before.
- **Charge for the pilot, and credit it.** A paid pilot credited to year one is lighter than a free pilot followed by a surprise price.
- **Transfer the risk.** An outcome-linked fee or a performance guarantee moves risk from the buyer to you. Price that risk honestly.
- **Change what kind of purchase it is.** In robotics, robots-as-a-service can turn a capital purchase into an operating expense. That often means a different approver and a shorter path.

Each of these makes the same force go further.

## Law 3: Compounding friction

Every handoff leaks energy. Fix the seams before you buy more pipeline.

Friction compounds because handoffs chain. Marketing to sales. Sales to solutions. Solutions to security. Security to procurement. Procurement to legal. Each one drops a little context and adds a little delay. Stack them and a warm buyer goes cold without anyone saying no.

My audit is simple. Take your last ten deals, won and lost. For each one, list every handoff and how many days the deal waited there. Then find the step where deals wait longest and most often. That's the seam to fix first. In my experience it's rarely the one the team expected.

## Time-to-Proof: the shaft through all five

If I could keep one number, it would be **Time-to-Proof**: days from the first qualified conversation to the buyer's first verifiable, buyer-agreed outcome on their own data, floor or workflow.

It runs through every variable. Vague direction makes it longer, because the buyer doesn't know what to test. Heavy mass makes it longer, because approvals take time. Weak force makes it irrelevant. Friction adds days at every seam. And without momentum, nobody is watching the clock.

To measure it, start the clock at the first conversation where the buyer has a real problem and a reason to solve it this year. Stop it when the buyer, not you, agrees that a result happened. Track the median across deals, not your best case.

It's a working metric because a team can move it this month. Revenue lags by quarters. Time-to-Proof moves the week you shrink a pilot or get the decision-maker to kickoff.

## Score your company

Score each variable from 1 to 5, and be honest. Start with the lowest score, even when it isn't the most interesting one.

| Variable | The question | At 1 | At 5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direction | Can a buyer explain why you, in one sentence, with you out of the room? | Every rep pitches it differently | Buyers repeat it back unprompted |
| Mass | How heavy is the decision? | Custom quotes, free endless pilots | A paid first step, approvable this quarter |
| Force | How much qualified attention do you create each week? | The founder's network | Weekly, measured, not tied to launches |
| Friction | Where does a deal sit and wait? | Nobody knows; the CRM is a graveyard | Every step owned, every kit ready |
| Momentum | Does it still work when the founder's on a plane? | Nothing closes without the founder | Runs without the founder, measured weekly |

A perfect 25 isn't the goal. Knowing which variable is a 1 is.

## What it isn't

Revenue Physics isn't a replacement for the frameworks you already use. It's a diagnostic. It tells you where the energy is going, so you spend the next quarter on the variable that's actually holding revenue back.

Most companies I meet don't have a pipeline problem. They have a direction problem that looks like a pipeline problem, or a mass problem that looks like a sales problem. The physics just makes that easier to see.
