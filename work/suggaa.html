00/ The brief

Three apps had to ship in lockstep. There was no design language — and no design team.

A ride-hailing product is really three products pretending to be one: a rider app, a driver app, and an operator console watching over both. Every flow crosses all three — a booking made here becomes a job offer there and a dispatch record somewhere else. When I joined in 2018 as the first design hire, none of it existed.

And the market was unforgiving: we were putting bikes, autos, totos and sedans on Indian streets against companies with thousand-person teams. The bet was focus — win the tier-2 cities the giants treated as afterthoughts, and win them with honesty: what you see is what you pay.

01/ The system — one language for three apps

Before the apps, the grammar.

The only way a small team ships three synchronized apps is to stop designing screens and start designing the language they're written in. Design System 2.0 is that language: tokens for every colour, space and type decision, and a component library that made the rider app, the driver app and the Fleet console agree by default instead of by heroics.

System 2.0 was crafted by the design duo I hired and led — Shubhanshu Singh & Latika Bhawnani — under my direction. Building the team that could build the system was the job.

02/ The rider app — book in seconds

City, outstation or rental — request in seconds, ride in minutes.

The rider app carries the whole brand promise on one screen: pick a city ride, an outstation trip or a rental; pick your vehicle — bike, auto, toto, mini or sedan; see the real fare before you commit. Under it sit the flows that make it hold up in the real world: 40+ onboarding and login states, favourites, booking for someone else, multi-stop rides, scheduling, and cancellation paths designed for both sides of the trip.

03/ The Saathi app — the driver's side

Riders feel the brand. Drivers keep it alive.

Saathi — the driver app — is where ride-hailing is actually won or lost. Drivers live in this screen ten hours a day, so it optimises for what they check between rides: today's earnings against a daily target they set themselves, payments that reconcile cleanly, and onboarding a first-time gig worker can finish without a helpdesk. Same design language as the rider app — because a driver who feels like a second-class user drives for someone else.

04/ The website — the front door

The pitch, the proof, and the hiring pipeline.

The 2024 website had three jobs: convert riders ("request in seconds, ride in minutes"), recruit Saathis, and carry the city-by-city expansion story — Jamshedpur and Ranchi live, Bengaluru and Delhi next. Designed responsive across desktop, tablet and mobile, with the careers flow and blog built in.

05/ Outcome — and the honest version

One operator, end to end — from blank canvas to product on the road.

Suggaa reached 25,000+ riders across two cities. The design system meant new features and new cities shipped without the experience drifting — three apps, five years, one language. And the team I built could carry it without me in the room, which is the only real test of a design leader.

What I'd do differently: build the system earlier. Version 1 was screens first, grammar later — and we paid for that in rework when the driver app scaled. System 2.0 fixed it with tokens and 40+ components in eight weeks, but the lesson stuck: on a multi-app product the design system isn't a deliverable, it's the foundation you pour first. It's why every 0→1 I run now starts with the language, not the screens.
